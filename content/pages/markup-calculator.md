# Markup Calculator

A markup calculator finds a selling price by adding a percentage of cost to that cost. Markup is profit divided by cost, while gross margin is profit divided by selling price. This calculator works in both directions, converts markup to margin, and shows profit after discounts, sales tax, and quantity.

## How to Use the Markup Calculator

Choose the tab that matches what you know, enter values, and select Calculate.

- **Price From Markup** takes a cost and a markup percentage and returns the selling price.
- **Markup From Price** takes a cost and a selling price and returns the markup and margin.
- **Price From Margin** takes a cost and a target gross margin and returns the price that achieves it.
- **Markup and Margin** converts one percentage into the other without any prices.

Open Quantity, discount, and sales tax to model a promotion or an order. Load example fills in a retail product with a discount and tax.

## Markup Formulas

| Result | Formula |
|---|---|
| Selling price | Cost × (1 + markup ÷ 100) |
| Markup | (Price - cost) ÷ cost × 100 |
| Gross margin | (Price - cost) ÷ price × 100 |
| Price from margin | Cost ÷ (1 - margin ÷ 100) |
| Markup to margin | Markup ÷ (100 + markup) × 100 |
| Margin to markup | Margin ÷ (100 - margin) × 100 |

Discounts apply to the selling price before tax. Sales tax is added for the customer but is not counted as profit, because it is collected on behalf of a tax authority.

## Markup Versus Margin

Markup and margin describe the same profit against different bases. Markup compares profit with cost, so it can exceed 100%. Margin compares profit with the selling price, so it always stays below 100% while the price is positive.

The gap matters when you set prices. A 50% markup produces a 33.33% margin, not a 50% margin. To reach a 50% margin, you need a 100% markup. Corporate Finance Institute's [markup definition](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/accounting/markup/) uses the same cost-based formula, and the [gross margin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_margin) entry explains the price-based measure.

| Markup | Gross margin |
|---|---:|
| 25% | 20.00% |
| 50% | 33.33% |
| 100% | 50.00% |
| 200% | 66.67% |

## Worked Example

A retailer buys a product for $40 and applies a 50% markup. The selling price is $40 × 1.5 = $60, so gross profit is $20 per item and gross margin is 33.33%.

During a sale, the retailer offers 10% off. The price falls to $54, profit falls to $14, and margin drops to 25.93%. With 8% sales tax, the customer pays $58.32, of which $4.32 is tax. Selling 100 units at the sale price earns $1,400 of gross profit.

## Choosing a Markup for Different Businesses

The right markup depends on what the cost figure includes and what the business must still pay from gross profit.

- **Retail and wholesale** usually mark up the landed cost of goods, including freight and duties.
- **Service businesses** often mark up a loaded labor cost that already includes benefits and overhead.
- **Software and SaaS** products have low unit costs, so teams tend to price on value and track margin instead. Recurring pricing structures are explained in [what B2B SaaS is](/saas/what-is-b2b-saas/).
- **Resellers** of hardware and licenses often work on thin markups, so small discounts can erase profit.

Before choosing a markup, confirm which costs are included. A markup on product cost alone can look healthy while overhead, payment fees, and returns remain uncovered. Small teams that track deals in a CRM can store target markups by product line, and Stack Row's review of [CRM software for small companies](/business-technology/crm-software-small-companies/) compares tools that handle quotes.

## Methodology and Limitations

- The calculator uses the standard cost-based markup and price-based gross margin formulas shown above.
- Results are rounded for display. Calculations keep full precision internally.
- Gross profit excludes operating expenses, payment processing, shipping to customers, and returns.
- Sales tax rules vary by location and product. The tax field applies one flat rate to the discounted price.
- Currency selection changes the displayed symbol only and does not convert values.
- Inputs stay in your browser. A calculation is saved locally after you select Calculate, and Reset calculator or Forget saved calculation removes it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Is the Formula for Markup?

Markup equals selling price minus cost, divided by cost, multiplied by 100. A product that costs $40 and sells for $60 has a 50% markup.

### How Do I Calculate Selling Price From Markup?

Multiply cost by one plus the markup expressed as a decimal. A $40 cost with a 50% markup gives $40 × 1.5 = $60.

### Is a 50% Markup the Same as a 50% Margin?

No. A 50% markup gives a 33.33% margin. A 50% margin needs a 100% markup, because margin is measured against the higher selling price.

### How Do I Convert Margin to Markup?

Divide the margin by 100 minus the margin, then multiply by 100. A 40% margin equals 40 ÷ 60 × 100, or a 66.67% markup.

### Can Markup Be More Than 100%?

Yes. Markup compares profit with cost, so any price above double the cost produces a markup above 100%. Margin cannot reach 100% while there is any cost.

### Should Sales Tax Be Included in Markup?

No. Sales tax is collected for a tax authority, so it is not revenue or profit. Calculate markup on the pre-tax price, then add tax for the customer.
